# What If It Snowed for 30 Days Straight?
Channel: Day Zero Tales (US) • Format: Day 0 → Day 30 • Target: ~5 min

## SCRIPT (narration, ~720 words, ~4.5-5 min)

It starts on a Monday morning. A few soft flakes drift past your window. Thirty days later, you'll be climbing out of your second-floor window... because the snow is taller than your front door.

This is Day Zero.

You live in a normal American suburb. Two-story house. One car. One shovel. The forecast says "light snow." Nobody's worried.

Day one. By evening there's a foot of snow on the ground. Schools close. Kids are building snowmen. Your neighbor posts a picture of his dog jumping into a drift. Honestly? It's kind of beautiful.

Day two. It doesn't stop. Two feet. Snowplows run all night, but every road they clear is white again an hour later. Your car is now a soft white lump in the driveway. You shovel the path. By lunch, it's gone.

Day three. Three feet. The grocery store is packed. Bread, milk, eggs, batteries, all gone. And if you watched our last video, you already know why. When the trucks can't move, the shelves can't refill.

Day five. The first big problem arrives. It's not the snow. It's the weight. Wet snow clings to power lines and tree branches. Somewhere down the street, a branch snaps and takes the line with it. The lights flicker... and go out. Across the region, hundreds of thousands of homes go dark. No power means no furnace fan. You light the fireplace and pile on blankets.

And here's something most people don't know. During the Buffalo blizzard in twenty twenty-two, snow piled up over a house's outdoor furnace vent. Carbon monoxide backed up inside. A man died in his own home. So you grab your shovel and dig out every vent on the house. Twice a day.

Day seven. Your house starts to creak. One inch of snow weighs roughly one pound on every square foot of your roof. A typical roof is built to handle about twenty. You now have more than seventy inches up there. You climb into the attic and hear the rafters groan. Time to clear the roof, very, very carefully.

Day ten. The snow is above your first-floor windows. Your living room is permanently dark, like a cave. The front door opens onto a solid white wall. So you start using the upstairs window as your new front door. Your neighbors dig tunnels between houses. Someone puts up a hand-painted sign: "Snow Street. Population: us."

Day fourteen. The whole country is feeling it now. Airports are closed. Highways disappear. Big flat roofs, on warehouses, malls, and gyms, start to cave in. Water pipes freeze and burst inside empty buildings. The National Guard drops food and medicine by helicopter. You wave from your window like you're on a deserted island.

Day twenty. Something strange happens. Your house actually feels warmer. That's because snow is full of trapped air, which makes it a great insulator. It's the same reason an igloo works. Outside, the wind is brutal. But inside your little snow cave, the temperature holds steady. The snow that's trapping you... is also protecting you.

Day twenty-five. You might think this could never really happen. But in January nineteen eleven, a tiny mountain camp in California called Tamarack got three hundred and ninety inches of snow in a single month. That's more than thirty-two feet. A three-story building, buried. It's still the United States record.

Day thirty. You wake up and something is different. It's quiet. The snow has stopped. And for the first time in a month, the sun comes out. Everyone climbs onto their roofs and cheers. It's over.

Except... it isn't. Because all that snow is about to become water. Thirty days of snow can hold several feet of water. When it melts, rivers overflow, basements flood, and roads turn into streams. The snow took a month to fall. The flood comes in a few days.

But your neighborhood gets through it. The same people who dug tunnels together now pump out each other's basements. And by spring, your kids are already asking when it's going to snow again.

So the next time you see that first flake drift past your window, enjoy it. It's beautiful. Just maybe... buy a second shovel.

What would you do on Day Ten? Tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because next time, we're going somewhere even colder.
