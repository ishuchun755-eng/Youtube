At six A.M. on a Monday, every truck in America stops. Not one engine turns over. Nobody knows why. And by Friday, you won't be able to buy a loaf of bread.

This is Day Zero.

You probably never think about trucks. They're just the big, loud thing you pass on the highway. But here's the strange part. Almost three quarters of everything shipped inside the United States, by weight, rides on a truck. Your food. Your medicine. Your gas. The toilet paper in your bathroom. At some point, every one of those things was in the back of a trailer.

And today, all of them are parked.

Hour one. Nothing seems wrong. You wake up, make coffee, check your phone. A few posts say the highways look weirdly empty. Someone jokes that it feels like a holiday. Millions of truck drivers are sitting in cabs that won't move, at truck stops and loading docks from Maine to California. But you don't know that yet.

Hour twelve. The news picks it up. "Nationwide trucking shutdown." No explanation. And that's when people start doing the one thing that makes everything worse. They go shopping.

Day one. Gas stations get their fuel by truck, often several times a week. Industry estimates say stations start running dry in just one to two days. By evening, the lines at the pumps wrap around the block. Pump handles are covered in plastic bags. Signs go up. "No gas. Sorry."

You fill up your tank anyway, just in case. So does everyone else.

Day two. Hospitals don't keep months of supplies on the shelf. Most of them run on "just in time" delivery. Gloves, syringes, IV bags, and some medicines arrive almost every day. Without trucks, hospitals start to ration. Surgeries that can wait, get postponed. Nurses start counting boxes.

Meanwhile, your phone keeps buzzing. The package you ordered says "delayed." Then "delayed" again. Then nothing at all.

Day three. This is the day you notice. Grocery stores don't have giant warehouses in the back anymore. The shelves you see are the warehouse. Most of it gets restocked by truck, overnight.

Trucking industry reports warn that serious food shortages can begin in about three days. And that's without panic. With panic, it's faster.

You walk into your local store. The bread aisle is empty. Milk, gone. Eggs, gone. Somehow, even the weird flavors of chips are gone. The only thing left is a single can of beets. And two people are staring at it.

Day four. Trash. Nobody thinks about trash until it stops leaving. Garbage trucks are trucks. Bags pile up on the curb. Then on the sidewalk. A big city makes thousands of tons of garbage every single day. By day four, downtown smells like a gym bag that was left in a hot car for a week.

Day five. Cash starts to disappear. ATMs are refilled by armored trucks. When the machines run empty, they stay empty. And small towns get hit the hardest. Many of them have no railroad, no port, and no airport. The only way anything arrives is by road.

Day seven. One week in. Factories shut down, because their parts are stuck in trailers somewhere in Nebraska. Car plants, food plants, paper mills. All silent. Millions of people are sent home from work. Farms are in trouble too. Cows still need feed. Chickens still need feed. And milk that can't be picked up gets poured down the drain.

Day ten. Hospitals face a scarier problem. Some reports warn that medical oxygen could start running low in one to two weeks. Oxygen is delivered by truck. And for patients who depend on it, this is no longer an inconvenience.

Day fourteen. Now, the water. You turn on the tap, and water still comes out. But the chemicals that make it safe to drink are delivered to treatment plants by truck, often every week or two. Without them, cities start issuing boil water notices. Then they ask people to save every drop.

And here's the strangest part. By now, everyone knows exactly what's missing. Trucks. But you can't just replace them. Trains only go where the tracks go. Planes are expensive, and tiny compared to a trailer. Ships need ports. That last mile, from the warehouse to your neighborhood store, almost always happens on a truck.

Day thirty. One month. The country looks different. Neighbors trade canned food over the fence. People ride bikes around town, looking for gas that doesn't exist. Stores are boarded up. Your street has gone quiet, the way it only does after a big snowstorm. Except this snow never melts.

And then, at six A.M. on a Tuesday, you hear a sound you haven't heard in thirty days. A low rumble. A diesel engine. Somewhere down the highway, the first truck starts to move.

Now, look outside. Right now, while you're watching this, millions of truck drivers are on the road. Most of them are hauling something you'll touch this week. The bread in your kitchen. The medicine in your cabinet. The gas in your car.

You never think about them. That's the point. When they do their job right, you never have to.

But now you know. When trucks stop, America stops. And it only takes about three days.
